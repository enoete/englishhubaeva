The Narrative Essay — telling a story that matters
What makes a narrative different from a list?

A narrative is not a list of events. It is a living story with a character who wants something, faces a problem, reaches a crisis point, and comes out changed. Every sentence should create tension, build atmosphere, or reveal character. If a sentence does none of these things — cut it.

1. Introduction — set the scene, hook the reader

Introduce your character, the setting (time and place), and begin the story with a sentence that demands the reader keep going. Use an in-medias-res opening (drop into the middle of action) or a striking image.

"The afternoon Cookie squeezed through the gate and vanished into the cane fields was the longest thirty minutes of my life."
2. Rising Action — build tension with layers

Introduce the problem and make it grow. Use your senses — what do you see, hear, smell, feel? Layer in obstacles. Let your character try and fail before succeeding. Add dialogue to reveal emotion.

"I called her name until my throat ached. Kreme sat by the fence, her ears flat, watching me with big brown worried eyes. The sun was dropping and the cane was tall and I had no idea which direction she had gone."
3. Climax — the peak, the crisis, the turning point

This is the most intense moment. Use short, punchy sentences. Let the emotion peak here. The reader must feel the urgency.

"Then I heard it — one sharp, scared bark from somewhere deep in the cane. My heart stopped."
4. Falling Action — things begin to resolve

The tension drops. The problem starts to be solved. The pace slows slightly. Describe how the situation changes.

"I pushed through two rows of cane and found her sitting in the middle of a clearing, tail wagging, a cane leaf stuck in her ear, looking completely satisfied with herself."
5. Resolution — what changed, what you learned

End with meaning. What did the experience teach you? How are you or the world different now? The last sentence of a narrative is often the most important. Make it count.

"That night, as Cookie curled up against my legs, I understood something I had not before: love is terrifying precisely because it is real, and real things can be lost."
The Persuasive Essay — 6-paragraph structure (from your notes)
The goal: convince your reader your position is correct

A persuasive essay presents a logical argument using evidence, examples, and reasoning. It acknowledges the other side (counter claim) and then dismantles it. Your tone must be formal — no "I, you, he, she, me, him, her" in body paragraphs.

Paragraph 1 — Introduction (3 parts)

Hook: A statistic, surprising fact, or short anecdote (3 sentences). Grab attention immediately.
Background: Show you have researched the topic. 1–2 sentences of context.
Thesis: State your position AND your three reasons in one clear sentence.

"It is argued that art and music should be mandatory in all schools because they improve academic performance, protect the mental health of students, and preserve the cultural identity of communities."
Paragraphs 2, 3, 4 — Body Paragraphs (3 parts each)

Topic Sentence: State one argument clearly. Use a connector to open.
Three pieces of evidence: Facts, statistics, examples, expert opinions — each one supporting the topic sentence.
Connection: The LAST sentence links your evidence back to your thesis. Never skip this.

Paragraph 5 — Counter Claim (3 parts)

Topic Sentence: Open with "Admittedly…" or "It is true that…" — acknowledge the other side respectfully.
Their 2–3 arguments: Be fair. State the opposing arguments properly.
Refutation: Show why those arguments are flawed or insufficient. Start with "However…" or "Nevertheless…"

Paragraph 6 — Conclusion (3 parts)

Restate stance: Begin with "In conclusion…" or "To summarise…" — do NOT use the same words as your thesis.
Brief summary: Recap each of your three points in one sentence each.
Call to action: What should the reader, school, government, or society do? End with purpose.

Connectors — from your own notes
First paragraph openers
  • To begin / To start off / Firstly / Initially / At first glance / In the first place
Body paragraph connectors
  • Furthermore / Additionally / In addition to / Equally important / Secondly / Next
  • To continue / In continuity / To move on / Moreover / By the same token
Counter claim openers
  • Admittedly… / It is true that… / While some may argue… / It must be acknowledged that…
Conclusion openers
  • To conclude / In conclusion / To summarise / In summary / To sum up / To end / Finally / To this end
Persuasive power phrases
  • It is evident that… / Research clearly shows… / Studies have found… / It is undeniable that…
  • Without question… / It is widely accepted that… / According to [source]…
  • Therefore… / As a result… / Consequently… / This demonstrates that…
  • However… / Nevertheless… / Despite this… / On the contrary… / In contrast…
Critical rule — the connection sentence

The last sentence of every body paragraph must connect your evidence back to your topic sentence and thesis. It answers: "So what? Why does this prove my argument?" Use: "This proves that… / Therefore it is clear that… / This demonstrates that…"

Descriptive techniques — make your writing vivid
Show, Don't Tell

Telling (weak): She was scared.
Showing (strong): Her palms had gone cold. She pressed them flat against her thighs to stop them shaking.

Five Senses — use at least two per scene

SIGHT: colours, shapes, movement, light
SOUND: what you hear, how loud, how close
SMELL: sharp, sweet, rotting, fresh
TOUCH: temperature, texture, pain, comfort
TASTE: sweet, bitter, salty, metallic

"The market smelled of ripe sapodilla and something frying in coconut oil. Stalls blazed in primary colours — tomatoes like rubies, yellow-green limes piled high, dark purple aubergines in a heap."
Simile — comparing with "like" or "as"
"The sea lay flat as hammered silver." / "She sang like someone who had been holding the note inside her whole life."
Metaphor — saying something IS something else
"The stage was a country she had always wanted to visit." / "The applause was a wave breaking over her."
Personification — giving human qualities to non-human things
"The wind gossiped through the coconut palms." / "The old church watched the village from the hilltop."
Dialogue — let your characters speak

Dialogue makes a story feel alive. Every new speaker gets a new paragraph. Use speech verbs beyond "said": whispered, demanded, muttered, insisted, called out.

"Dad," I whispered, "do you think we made a mistake?"
He was quiet for a long time.
"We made a choice," he said finally. "Those are different things."
Short sentences for tension

When the action peaks — use short sentences. They create pace and urgency.

"I ran. The cane closed behind me. I couldn't see her. I couldn't hear anything. Then — silence."

The Last Rehearsal

A short story

The night before the Junior Calypso Monarch competition, Nadia could not sleep. She lay on her back staring at the ceiling fan as it made its slow circles in the dark, and she went through her song for the seventeenth time in her head. She had written it herself — every word — about the sea. About what happened to it when people stopped caring.

Her mother had tried to comfort her at dinner. "You know it, baby," she had said, placing a bowl of pelau in front of her. "You know every single word." Nadia had nodded and eaten in silence and gone to bed at nine, as if an early night could somehow make the morning arrive better prepared.

But at two in the morning she was still awake, and she had begun to doubt herself in that slow, corrosive way that doubt has when there is nothing else to occupy the mind. Maybe the other contestants had better songs. Maybe her voice would crack at the crucial moment, the way it had at the Christmas concert in Form Two. Maybe the judges would not care about the sea.

She got up, walked quietly to the kitchen, and made herself a cup of hot chocolate the way her father used to make it — two sugars, a pinch of cinnamon, stirred exactly twelve times. He had been gone three years, but his recipe remained. She sat at the kitchen table and drank it slowly, and somewhere in the warmth of it she found something that was not quite courage but was close enough to function as one.

In the morning, she stood in the wings of the stage at the Cultural Centre, watching the contestant before her finish to generous applause. She was twelve years old, five feet tall, and holding a song she had built from scratch. When the compere called her name, she walked out into the white light and opened her mouth.

She did not win first place. She came second. But as she walked off the stage with her runner-up ribbon, the head judge — a woman with short silver hair who had been adjudicating Calypso competitions for thirty years — caught her by the arm.

"That song," the woman said quietly. "Who wrote it?"

"I did," said Nadia.

The woman looked at her for a long moment. "Keep writing," she said. "You have something."

On the drive home, Nadia held the ribbon in her lap and watched the island go by through the window — the cane fields, the old stone walls, the sea glinting in the distance. She thought about what "something" meant. She thought it might take her a long time to find out. She decided that was all right.

Glossary: corrosive — slowly damaging or destroying something; compere — the host who announces performers; adjudicating — judging a competition officially

📝 Passage 2 — Non-Fiction (Informational)  |  Total: 35 marks  |  Estimated time: 45 minutes

The Steel Pan: Born in the Rubbish Heap, Heard Around the World

Informational article

Of all the musical instruments ever invented, the steel pan is perhaps the most unlikely. It was not designed in a laboratory or crafted by a master luthier in a European workshop. It was born in the slums of Port of Spain, Trinidad, in the 1930s and 1940s, hammered into existence by young men who had been banned from playing drums and were determined to make music anyway.

The story begins with the British colonial government. Alarmed by the power of drumming to unite communities — particularly during Carnival — the authorities banned African drums in Trinidad in 1884. For decades, the population found workarounds: bamboo percussion groups called tamboo bamboo filled the void, creating complex rhythms by striking lengths of bamboo on the ground and against each other. When bamboo was also eventually restricted, musicians turned to whatever they could find: dustbin lids, biscuit tins, car parts.

Somewhere around 1939, a musician from the Laventille neighbourhood of Port of Spain discovered that a paint tin which had been left in the sun and dented in a particular way could produce different pitches depending on where it was struck. This observation — that a concave metal surface, properly shaped, could become a tuned musical instrument — was the breakthrough that changed everything.

Over the following decades, the steel pan evolved rapidly. Craftsmen called "tuners" developed techniques for hammering oil drum bottoms into the curved, divided surfaces we recognise today. By the 1950s, steel bands were competing in Carnival, drawing enormous crowds. By the 1960s, the instrument had begun to travel internationally, introduced to Britain and North America by Caribbean immigrants.

Today, the steel pan is recognised as Trinidad and Tobago's national instrument and is played in orchestras, schools, and concert halls around the world. The London Symphony Orchestra has performed alongside steel bands. Universities in the United States offer degrees in pan performance. What began as an act of creative defiance in the face of colonial repression has become a globally celebrated art form.

Yet despite this success, the people who built this instrument — the panmen of Laventille and the East Dry River communities — remained largely poor and unrecognised for much of the twentieth century. The instrument travelled the world; its inventors often did not. It is a story that sits alongside the steel pan's triumph as an uncomfortable reminder that recognition and justice do not always arrive together.

Glossary: luthier — a craftsperson who makes stringed instruments; concave — curved inward like a bowl; defiance — bold resistance against authority; repression — using force to control people

📝 Passage 3 — The Challenge Passage  |  Total: 40 marks  |  Estimated time: 55 minutes  |  ⚠ Most difficult

Two Letters

A paired text exercise — read BOTH letters before answering any questions

Letter A: From a school principal to parents

Dear Parents and Guardians,

I am writing to inform you of a difficult but necessary decision made by the school board at our most recent meeting. With effect from the new academic year, the school will be discontinuing its Music and Visual Arts programmes. This decision has not been taken lightly. Faced with significant budgetary constraints and the requirement to improve our national examination results in Mathematics and English Language, the board has concluded that resources must be redirected toward core academic subjects.

We are aware that many students have benefited from these programmes, and we do not underestimate the value of creative expression. However, in the current climate, we believe this is the most responsible course of action. The school's primary obligation is to equip students with the qualifications they need to succeed in secondary school and beyond.

We thank you for your continued support and understanding.

Yours sincerely,
Mrs. A. Clarke, Principal


Letter B: Reply from a parent

Dear Mrs. Clarke,

I have read your letter with both sadness and frustration, and I feel compelled to respond.

My daughter has been in your Music programme for three years. In that time, I have watched her grow from a child who struggled to speak in front of more than two people into a young woman who recently performed before an audience of several hundred. Her confidence, her discipline, her ability to work under pressure — all of it came from music. I do not believe Mathematics gave her that. I believe music did.

I also want to challenge the assumption that academic results and creative education are in competition with each other. Research from Johns Hopkins University, Harvard, and numerous other institutions has shown consistently that students engaged in arts education perform better, not worse, in core academic subjects. You are not choosing between art and achievement. You are removing something that was helping both.

I understand that budgets are real and difficult. But I would ask you to consider: what is the cost of removing the one class where a shy child finds her voice? What is the cost of telling an entire generation of students that creativity has no place in their education? Some costs do not appear on a spreadsheet.

I am asking you, respectfully but firmly, to reconsider.

Yours sincerely,
A concerned parent

Glossary: budgetary constraints — limits on how much money can be spent; compelled — feeling strongly that you must do something; assumption — something believed to be true without proof
